**Original Question:**  
I will be teaching about food next class. The evidence supporting saltless diet looks weak from a scientific point of view, and could also increase health risk, as appears from the following discussion. (The results showing harm from salt are mostly based on normal consumption levels, which are clearly too high.) When I read first read about results showing a U-shaped relationship between salt consumption and mortality/other bad effects, I assumed it was due to reverse causality (and some studies showing this, indeed, were flawed). But the following paper appears to show that there is negative impact on people who have experienced heart failure. Since all heart failure patients are advised to restrict salt, there is less likelihood of reverse causality here. I used nature research assistant to summarize, because biology type papers can be hard to understand.

[2/16/26, 10:09:06 PM] Ashok: Salt restriction and risk of adverse outcomes in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction



https://todayspractitioner.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Salt-Restriction-and-Heart-Study.pdf



Nature research assistant summary: 



This article investigates the relationship between cooking salt restriction and clinical outcomes in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Previously, guidelines recommended sodium restriction to manage heart failure, but evidence was mixed, especially for HFpEF, and most studies excluded this subgroup. The authors analyzed data from the TOPCAT trial, focusing on self-reported cooking salt intake, and found that overstrict salt restriction (nearly no salt added during cooking) was associated with worse outcomes, including higher risks of the primary composite endpoint and heart failure hospitalization. They also observed that this association was more pronounced in younger and non-white patients. These findings suggest that excessively strict salt restriction may not be beneficial and highlight the need for further research to determine optimal salt intake levels for HFpEF patients.

[2/16/26, 10:10:02 PM] Ashok: I came acorss the following paper today, which is more worrying, because it considers more general populations: 2. Associations of urinary sodium excretion with cardiovascular events in individuals with and without hypertension: a pooled analysis of data from four studies



https://tinyurl.com/5fspv8ez



Nature research assistant summary: 



This article investigates the relationship between urinary sodium excretion and cardiovascular events and mortality, focusing on whether hypertension modifies this association. The authors pooled data from four large prospective studies involving over 133,000 individuals across multiple countries, estimating sodium intake via 24-hour urinary sodium excretion. They found a U-shaped association in hypertensive individuals, with increased risks at both low (<3 g/day) and high (≥7 g/day) sodium intakes, even after adjusting for blood pressure. In contrast, in normotensive individuals, only low sodium intake was associated with increased risk, while high intake showed no significant association. These findings suggest that sodium reduction efforts might be most relevant for hypertensive populations consuming high-sodium diets, providing new insight into how hypertension status influences sodium-related cardiovascular risk.

**Full Answer:**  
It is not the case that all or even most yogiis eat a saltless diet, but many do. And of course there is a wide range of people who consider themselves "yogiis". So it all depends on who we include within this classification. But there is certainly a long-standing tradition within the yoga community since ancient times who do not take salt.

- The WFPB no SOS is increasingly accepted in both the medical and the general society in the USA as well as around the world. Of course, still a tiny percentage of the society at large.
