**Original Question:**  
When we covered it in class, I did not really get why we would want to measure brahma. (a) Is the main point of this that we want to show that it is beyond the mind? That is, the problem is not just that it cannot be measured, but it cannot be comprehended by the mind. (b) Or is the purpose of the discussion to actually show that it is infinite.



In paragraph 9, we show that the mind cannot comprehend the unit consciousness. Paragraph 10 mentions that 'On withdrawing mind (ahamtattva) to its limits we still find something beyond it ... . Creation thus exists beyond the limit of the mind.' I take it that unit consciousness is shuddha purusha and so not a part of the creation. The only part of creation we have shown the ahamtattva not reaching is the mahattattva. So, am I right in understanding that the part of creation beyond the mind, mentioned here, is mahattattva, in the sense that it is beyond the ahamtattva?



I am not sure I understand the argument showing that brahma must be infinite in paragraph 10. Paragraph 9 ends with 'Mind can never think, feel or grasp any idea of the size of Brahma.' We are then presenting an argument (which uses our mind) to argue in paragraph 10 that Brahma must be infinite. This appears to contradict the claim that mind cannot grasp the size of Brahma. Is the claim in paragraph 9 limited to direct perception, while the argument in 10 uses anumana? So, through anumana, we can figure out the Brahma must be infinite?



I guess I don't follow the anumana either. We first show that creation is vishala. We then say that '... _[a]_ if a part can be vishala, _[b]_ Brahma, the whole which contains it, must be infinite or virat'. I don't see why [a]  implies [b]. Is it meant to create the feeling of the vastness of Brahma rather than formally proving that it is infinite?





**Full Answer:**  
So as the background for all your questions, it is important to take a step back and see what this chapter is about and what its goal is. The title is "What is the Cosmic Entity", and its goal is quite ambitious. The first chapter concluded having reached the point of showing through the process of seeing a book that Brahma does indeed exist and is the multiple of all the unit consciousnesses which the student has come to learn about in the book-seeing prakriya. 



So now that we understand that Brahma exists and have a very basic idea about it, the next step is to characterize it further. As we discussed earlier, this series of karapatrams is made as an introduction for those wishing to engage in the yogic life and thus needing to understand the yogic outlook. Everyone comes to the doorway of yoga carrying all sorts of dogmas from their upbringing, their family, and the greater society in which they live. Helping them to move beyond the limited conceptions of God/Allah/Bhagavan which they grew up with is the goal here. Those who grew up in the Hindu faith inevitably have some concept of Bhagavan in the form of some murti. Moving from that to the reality of what Brahma actually is, is the topic of this karapatram. And the medium for this movement is logic. Whereas Brahma is above and beyond karya-karana bhava. As such it is a goal which is ultimately by definition going to have certain limitations. We know that the only way to come to know Brahma is through sadhana. Even then, the path of rationality and logic for those who have an open mind is incredibly valuable. It gives them a pathway for ultimately letting go of all the images and pictures they grew up with and embracing the life that will lead them to realization of the Infinite Entity. 



So in this chapter two, in successive paragraphs, we get the characterization of Brahma as:



- having no shape or form

- having no beginning or root cause. 

- being non-causal

- being absolutely independent

- the unitary combination of Puruśa and Prakrti which are both subtle. 

- the collective name for these two and thus itself subtle and something which can only be appreciated in the final stage of the mind’s merger in its source. 



Then, with all the above as the context, we arrive to paragraphs 6-10, where your questions are.



At this point in the karapatram it has already been shown that Brahma has the characteristics enlisted above. Among those characteristics is that of being infinite kAlika-dRuShTyA. So the next step is to show that Brahma is infinite daeshika-dRuShTyA as well, and hence the discussion as to its measurement. If using the logic plane we want to consider whether something is infinite daeshika-dRuShTyA, then it is quite a natural step to try and measure its size. We are going from the known to the unknown, right. The students have really never thought seriously about what it means for something to be infinite daeshika-dRuShTyA. And they are quite familiar with the concept of measuring the size of things. Indeed everything they are familiar with in their lives is measurable. So let us see about measuring Brahma. In the process of considering what the steps might be to measure it, we reach the conclusion that, in contrast to everything we know in the world, the size of Brahma cannot be measured. There is not even a technique that could be considered for doing so, as is discussed in detail in the karapatram. So yes, the purpose of the discussion to actually show through the pathway of logic and reasoning that, unlike all the things we are familiar with in the world, Brahma is infinite daeshika-dRuShTyA. And the implication is that nothing which is measurable-- such as a painting or a statue-- can be used to represent It.



We have already reached the conclusion that Brahma is infinite kAlika-dRuShTyA (i.e. without beginning), and now we are considering whether it is infinite daeshika-dRuShTyA (i.e. does it have a measurable end).
